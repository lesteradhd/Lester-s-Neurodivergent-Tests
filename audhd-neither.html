Low Traits

// The scan came back quiet.

Your scores didn't show strong ADHD or autistic patterns. That might mean several things — and this page explores them honestly, along with what understanding neurodivergence actually gives you whether or not it's your own.

What a low traits result actually means

A low score on this kind of assessment can mean a few different things and it's worth being honest about all of them. The most straightforward: the ADHD and autistic traits this assessment measures aren't your dominant wiring. Your nervous system doesn't strongly match the patterns we were looking for. But there are other possibilities. Heavy masking — particularly in people who have been undiagnosed for a long time — can suppress scores because the assessment asks how you experience things and a deep masker's automatic response is the performed version, not the real one. If you've been masking long enough, you might score lower than your actual neurology because you've stopped having conscious access to the real responses. Third possibility: you're here because someone in your life is neurodivergent, not you. That's actually the most common reason people with low trait scores end up in these spaces — and it's just as valid a reason to be here.

What neurotypical people miss about neurodivergent experience

Most neurotypical people who care about neurodivergent people in their lives try to understand through the clinical lens — diagnosis criteria, accommodations lists, symptom management strategies. That information is useful but incomplete because it describes the outside of the experience without the inside. The inside is: the specific exhaustion of a brain that processes differently operating in a world that wasn't designed for it. The shame that accumulates from years of being told you're too much or not enough. The gap between how you appear to the world and how you actually feel. The grief of late diagnosis. The complex relationship with identity when you've spent years performing a version of yourself for survival. That inside view is what changes how you show up for the neurodivergent people in your life. Not strategies. The actual felt experience, described by people who live it.

The relationship between neurodivergent and neurotypical people

Neurodivergent and neurotypical people are not in opposition — but there is a real asymmetry in how much each group is required to adapt to the other. Neurodivergent people spend enormous amounts of energy learning to navigate neurotypical environments, norms, and communication styles. The reverse rarely happens at the same scale. This isn't about blame. Most of it isn't intentional. It's the result of neurotypical norms being so dominant that they're invisible to people who operate within them — like water to fish. The norms seem like just 'how things work' rather than a specific set of rules built for a specific type of brain. When a neurotypical person genuinely learns to see those norms as norms — rather than universal standards — and adjusts their approach accordingly, the quality of their relationships with neurodivergent people changes dramatically. Not because they're doing more. Because they're doing something different.

// PRACTICAL TOOLS + PERSPECTIVE SHIFTS
TIP 1 — STOP APPLYING NEUROTYPICAL LOGIC TO NEURODIVERGENT BEHAVIOUR
When a neurodivergent person in your life does something that seems irrational — shuts down in a situation that seems manageable, reacts intensely to something that seems minor, can't follow through on something they genuinely want to do — ask 'what might be happening in their nervous system right now' before asking 'why are they doing this to me.'
Neurodivergent behaviour almost always has a neurological logic. It's just not the same logic neurotypical brains use. When you can access that logic — even partially — behaviour that looked unreasonable starts to look like a coherent response to a different internal experience. That shift from 'unreasonable' to 'different logic' changes everything about how you respond.
TIP 2 — LEARN TO READ DYSREGULATION BEFORE IT PEAKS
Neurodivergent people often can't access words for what they're experiencing while they're experiencing it — especially autistic people in overwhelm and ADHD people in emotional flooding. Learn the specific early signs for the person in your life. Increased quiet. Shorter responses. Physical stillness or restlessness. When you see them — offer space, not questions.
The moment of peak dysregulation is the worst possible time for a productive conversation. It's also the moment most people try to have one. The conversation doesn't happen in the moment — it happens after recovery, in a calm window, when the nervous system is actually available. Timing is more than half the intervention.
TIP 3 — ASK WHAT HELPS — THEN BELIEVE THE ANSWER
Most neurodivergent people have been told their whole lives that what they say they need isn't really what they need. They've been second-guessed, overridden, and 'helped' in ways that didn't help. The single most supportive thing you can do is ask what actually helps — and then do that thing, even if it doesn't make sense to you.
Support that doesn't match what the person says they need isn't really support — it's support designed for a different person. The neurotypical impulse to fix, reframe, or redirect is often the least useful response. Presence and genuine belief in what someone tells you about their own experience is usually the most useful one.
