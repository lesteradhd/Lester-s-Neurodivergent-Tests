AuDHD

// Both systems running simultaneously.

You scored high on both ADHD and autistic traits. That means you're not just dealing with one neurological system — you're running two contradictory ones at the same time, every day. This page goes deeper into what that actually means and what actually helps.

Why AuDHD is a third thing, not just two things

Most people think AuDHD means having ADHD and autism side by side — two separate conditions stacked on top of each other. That's not quite right. What actually happens is that the two systems interact, mask each other, and produce a third experience that's distinct from either one alone. The ADHD brain craves novelty, impulsivity, and constant stimulation. The autistic brain needs predictability, deep processing, and controlled input. They don't just coexist — they actively contradict each other. The result is a nervous system that's simultaneously overstimulated and under-stimulated, simultaneously craving connection and overwhelmed by it, simultaneously needing to move and needing to stay still. This is why AuDHD people are so often misunderstood in both ADHD and autism spaces. The ADHD community doesn't fully account for the routine-seeking, the sensory sensitivity, the need for deep processing. The autism community doesn't fully account for the impulsivity, the dopamine-chasing, the emotional volatility. You're both and neither — and that specific experience needs its own language.

The masking that AuDHD produces

AuDHD people often develop extremely sophisticated masking because the two conditions mask each other in clinical settings — meaning you can look 'not ADHD enough' and 'not autistic enough' simultaneously to people who are looking for one or the other. What this produces is a person who is very good at appearing functional while running an enormous internal load. The autistic side builds detailed social scripts. The ADHD side uses charm and humour to cover the rest. Together they produce someone who can navigate most rooms while using far more cognitive resource than anyone around them realises. The cost is the same as it is for all heavy masking — chronic exhaustion, identity confusion, the feeling that nobody has ever seen the real thing — but compounded by the fact that the experience is so specific and contradictory that it's hard to explain even to people who are trying to understand.

What AuDHD burnout looks like

AuDHD burnout is not the same as ADHD burnout or autistic burnout, though it borrows from both. It can look like complete shutdown — flat affect, inability to initiate, nothing registering. Or it can look like explosion — emotional volatility, impulsive decisions, complete inability to manage sensory input. What makes it hard to recognise is that the two sides can take turns. You might have a shutdown period followed by a manic-seeming high-energy period — not because things are better but because a different system is now in the foreground. This cycling is often misread as mood disorder. The pattern to watch for is this: if shutdown or overwhelm consistently follows periods of high output or heavy social demand, that's the cost of running both systems beyond their capacity. It's not your personality fluctuating. It's your nervous system cycling through its recovery process.

// PRACTICAL TOOLS + PERSPECTIVE SHIFTS
TIP 1 — BUILD FLEXIBLE STRUCTURE — NOT RIGID, NOT CHAOTIC
Rigid schedules dysregulate the ADHD side through boredom and constraint. No structure dysregulates the autistic side through unpredictability and demand for constant decision-making. The solution is semi-structured days: fixed anchors (same wake time, same morning sequence, same wind-down) with flexible middles. Enough routine to feel safe, enough openness to stay engaged.
You are not inconsistent. You are a system that requires a very specific type of environment to function — one that most productivity advice doesn't account for because it was designed for brains that don't have two competing operating systems. Stop applying standard templates and start designing for your actual architecture.
TIP 2 — NAME WHICH SYSTEM IS ACTIVATED RIGHT NOW
When you're overwhelmed, ask one question: is this ADHD overwhelm (under-stimulated, restless, brain spinning, need movement and novelty) or autistic overwhelm (over-stimulated, too much input, need silence and withdrawal)? They need opposite responses. Getting fast at this question is one of the highest-leverage skills an AuDHD person can develop.
Your overwhelm isn't random. It has a source and a logic. When you can identify which system is in distress you stop responding to both the same way — which is what most coping strategies incorrectly tell you to do — and start giving each system what it actually needs.
TIP 3 — SUSTAINABILITY OVER CONSISTENCY
Stop tracking whether you were consistent today. Start tracking whether today was sustainable — whether you ended it with enough capacity to function tomorrow. Consistency is a neurotypical metric that your brain can't reliably hit. Sustainability is a neurodivergent metric that's actually achievable and tells you something real about how you're doing.
Consistency was never your goal. It was an external standard you absorbed from a world that assumed everyone's baseline was the same. Your baseline isn't the same. Measuring yourself against it is like measuring a fish by how well it climbs trees. Sustainability gives you a measure that's actually built for your biology.
