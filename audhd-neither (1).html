Low Traits

// The scan came back quiet.

Your scores didn't show strong ADHD or autistic patterns. That might mean several things — and this page explores them honestly, along with what understanding neurodivergence actually gives you whether or not it's your own wiring.

What a low traits result actually means

A low score can mean a few different things. The most straightforward: the ADHD and autistic traits this assessment measures aren't your dominant wiring. But there are other possibilities. Heavy masking — particularly in people who have been undiagnosed for a long time — can suppress scores because a deep masker's automatic response is the performed version, not the real one. If you've been masking long enough, you might score lower than your actual neurology. Third possibility: you're here because someone in your life is neurodivergent, not you. That's the most common reason people with low trait scores end up in these spaces — and it's just as valid a reason to be here.

What neurotypical people miss about neurodivergent experience

Most neurotypical people who care about neurodivergent people in their lives try to understand through the clinical lens — diagnosis criteria, accommodations lists, symptom management strategies. That information is useful but incomplete because it describes the outside of the experience without the inside. The inside is: the specific exhaustion of a brain that processes differently in a world that wasn't designed for it. The shame that accumulates from years of being told you're too much or not enough. The gap between how you appear to the world and how you actually feel. That inside view is what changes how you show up for the neurodivergent people in your life.

The asymmetry nobody talks about

Neurodivergent and neurotypical people are not in opposition — but there is a real asymmetry in how much each group is required to adapt to the other. Neurodivergent people spend enormous energy learning to navigate neurotypical environments, norms, and communication styles. The reverse rarely happens at the same scale. When a neurotypical person genuinely learns to see those norms as norms — rather than universal standards — and adjusts their approach accordingly, the quality of their relationships with neurodivergent people changes dramatically.

Practical Tools + Perspective Shifts

Whether you're neurodivergent yourself or supporting someone who is — understanding the real experience (not the clinical summary) is what changes everything. Tools help. Perspective changes the relationship.

Tip 1
Stop applying neurotypical logic to neurodivergent behaviour
When a neurodivergent person in your life does something that seems irrational — ask 'what might be happening in their nervous system right now' before asking 'why are they doing this to me.'
The Reframe
Neurodivergent behaviour almost always has a neurological logic. It's just not the same logic neurotypical brains use. When you access that logic — even partially — behaviour that looked unreasonable starts to look like a coherent response to a different internal experience.
Tip 2
Learn to read dysregulation before it peaks
Neurodivergent people often can't access words for what they're experiencing while they're experiencing it. Learn the specific early signs for the person in your life. When you see them — offer space, not questions.
The Reframe
The moment of peak dysregulation is the worst possible time for a productive conversation. It's also the moment most people try to have one. Timing is more than half the intervention.
Tip 3
Ask what helps — then believe the answer
Most neurodivergent people have been told their whole lives that what they say they need isn't really what they need. Ask what actually helps — and then do that thing, even if it doesn't make sense to you.
The Reframe
Support that doesn't match what the person says they need isn't really support — it's support designed for a different person. Presence and genuine belief in what someone tells you about their own experience is usually the most powerful thing you can offer.
